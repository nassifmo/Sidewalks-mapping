# Mapping Lebanon's Sidewalks Starts From a Blank Sheet

No public dataset anywhere in Lebanon records sidewalk geometry, width, curb height or condition at city scale. A research team will therefore have to **build the inventory itself**, using a few restricted Beirut datasets to get started. OpenStreetMap, the only open national source, has just **32 separately mapped sidewalks across 111,794 road ways, no sidewalk widths and 2 kerb nodes**. So OSM provides the street network to attach data to, but almost no sidewalk content. The useful existing material is all in Beirut and all needs permission. It consists of a 2010 AUB curbline layer for Municipal Beirut (6,805 polylines at 1:2,000), an MIT/AUB per-street estimate of sidewalk presence, width and obstruction for central and blast-affected neighbourhoods (2022–23), and open building footprints from the Beirut Urban Lab. No method on its own captures all the attributes the team needs. Overhead imagery can find sidewalks but cannot measure sub-metre widths at satellite resolution. Street-level imagery can measure width to about ±0.25 m and see poles and generators, but Google's terms bar building datasets from Street View. Only field measurement or handheld LiDAR records curb height. Three conditions shape the plan. Drone permits have been **suspended nationwide since 1 March 2026**. The whole country is under a US Level 4 advisory with strikes in Beirut this year. And Beirut's governor begins enforcing against sidewalk occupation on **16 November 2026**, which makes any obstruction survey sensitive to timing. The recommended methodology has four phases: a desk-built base network, a walking 360° imagery and field-audit pilot that also produces locally calibrated ground truth, model-assisted scale-up, and a stratified sample survey for cities outside Beirut.

## Only Beirut has anything to start from, and all of it is gated

OSM is effectively empty for this purpose. A national Overpass query run on 6 November 2026 returned **32 `footway=sidewalk` ways, zero ways carrying a sidewalk width, and only 211 of 111,794 drivable road ways with any `sidewalk*` tag**, most of them recording that there is *no* sidewalk ([Overpass API](https://overpass-api.de/api/interpreter)). Street furniture is similarly thin: 284 street lamps and 27 benches nationwide. That is far below reality, since a single 2010 layer counts 943 light poles in Municipal Beirut alone. The sidewalk edits come from about ten individual accounts, not an organised campaign, and the humanitarian mapping activations, such as the October 2024 conflict response, focused on buildings and roads ([OSM Wiki](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/ES:Lebanon:_Conflict_-_October_2024)). OSM and its HOT and WFP exports on HDX are still valuable as a topologically clean centreline network to split into sidewalk segments ([HDX](https://data.humdata.org/m/dataset/hotosm_lbn_sea_ports); [AmeriGEOSS](https://data.amerigeoss.org/dataset/lebanon-streets-and-pathways)).

The most valuable public-origin layer is a **2010 "Roads Curblines" layer for all of Municipal Beirut**: 6,805 polylines digitised at 1:2,000 by AUB's Faculty of Engineering and Architecture, catalogued with a 943-point street-light-pole layer and a 2011 centreline layer. The catalogue offers them "for academic and research purposes" and warns that some layers are student work of variable accuracy ([AUB Neighborhood Initiative GIS catalogue](https://scholarworks.aub.edu.lb/server/api/core/bitstreams/71b5ddf4-a555-4cf2-a70c-4bf0fc19da4a/content)). Curblines mark the road-side edge of the sidewalk. The Beirut Urban Lab's building footprints, available through the open-access Beirut Urban Observatory, mark the frontage edge ([civictech.guide](https://civictech.guide/listing/beirut-urban-observatory); [Dateno](https://dateno.io/registry/catalog/cdi00003253)). Measuring the distance between the two gives a first-pass built-width estimate for every street in Municipal Beirut at almost no cost. The estimate has two limits. It reflects 2010 conditions, before the 2020 port blast and the rebuilding that followed. And redistribution rights must be negotiated with AUB.

The closest thing to a real sidewalk inventory is the **MIT City Form Lab / AUB "Community Streets" dataset**. For each street in Karantina, Mar Mikhael, Gemmayzeh, Rmeil, Jeitaoui, Hamra and Raouché, the team "approximated sidewalk presence, width, and quality based on open street-view imagery analysis from Mapillary (Google Street View is very limited in Beirut), prior studies, and in-person observation." They found that **6% of streets had no sidewalk, about 17% of sidewalks had permanent obstructions, and about 20% were narrower than 1.2 m**. They also took pedestrian counts of 7 to 416 people per hour ([Sevtsuk et al., Community Streets report](https://edge.sitecorecloud.io/daralhandas9921-dargroupcor87ce-prod-3847/media/Project/dargroup/dargroup/Insights/Report_Community-Streets_Prof-Sevtsuk.pdf); [MIT DUSP](https://dusp.mit.edu/news/increasing-sidewalk-accessibility)). The underlying GIS is unpublished and its licence is unknown, so it must be requested from the authors. The widths are estimates, not survey measurements.

Everything else is qualitative or locked in PDFs. That includes the Dar "Beirut Open Space Damage Report" (2020), which records missing sidewalks and sidewalk parking ([Dar](https://dar.com/Content/Publications/PUD/Beirut-Open%20space%20damage%20report.pdf)); ACTED/REACH snapshots of Geitawi and Karm el Zeitoun ([ACTED](https://www.acted.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/urban-snapshot-geitawi-2021-06-02-final.pdf)); a Jeanne d'Arc Street walkability study ([Daleel Madani](https://daleel-madani.org/sites/default/files/Resources/6.Improving_Walkability_Beirut_Lessons_from_Jeanne_dArc_Street%5B1%5D.pdf)); AUB theses on Hamra ([AUB ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.aub.edu.lb/handle/10938/10036)); and UN-Habitat/UNICEF neighbourhood profiles for Tabbaneh (Tripoli), Old Saida and Maachouk (Tyre) ([UN-Habitat](https://unhabitat.org/sites/default/files/2020/11/un-habitat-unicef_old_saida_neighbourhood_profile.pdf)). Those profiles are the only plausible prior sources for cities outside Beirut, and whether they contain street-by-street sidewalk content is unverified. The research found no sidewalk layer published by CDR, DGU, the Ministry of Public Works, the Cadastre, or any municipality other than Beirut. Municipal engineering departments probably hold CAD as-builts for individual rehabilitation projects, but that is a lead to pursue through formal requests, not a confirmed dataset. The research also found **no measured curb-height or surface-condition data anywhere in Lebanon**, apart from two OSM surface tags. For Tripoli, Saida, Jounieh, Zahle and Tyre, a primary survey is unavoidable.

## Satellite imagery finds sidewalks but cannot measure them

Tile2Net is the reference open-source pipeline for extracting sidewalks from overhead imagery. It segments aerial tiles into sidewalk, crosswalk, road and background, then converts the masks into polygons and a routable network. Trained on 19 cm aerial imagery from Cambridge, Washington DC and New York, it reached **82.7% sidewalk IoU on test data**. On a city it never saw in training (Boston), its polygons covered 77.9% of the official sidewalk area ([Hosseini et al. 2023](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10493942)). It does not output width natively. Width has to be added by skeletonising the mask and doubling the distance from skeleton to edge ([Hosseini et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.06120)). The code is labelled beta and "not ready for production" ([GitHub](https://github.com/VIDA-NYU/tile2net)). Esri packages a derivative model that accepts 10–30 cm imagery but expects it to perform well only in a handful of US states ([Esri](https://doc.arcgis.com/en/pretrained-models/latest/imagery/introduction-to-pedestrian-infrastructure-classification-model.htm)). A third-party run on 30 cm imagery showed visible gaps, which the authors attributed to the coarser resolution, the unfamiliar context and tree cover ([Wherobots](https://docs.wherobots.com/tutorials/example-notebooks/rasterflow-tile2net)). No published test exists in any Middle Eastern or dense Mediterranean city. Beirut also lacks the municipal planimetric layers Tile2Net used as training labels, so the team would have to hand-correct masks to fine-tune it. The SAM-based GeoSAM reports at least 5% mIoU gains over earlier methods, but it too is trained on US data ([arXiv 2311.11319](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.11319v4)).

Resolution caps what overhead imagery can do. If each edge of the sidewalk is located to within one pixel, width uncertainty is roughly ±0.6 m at 30 cm ground sample distance (GSD), ±0.3 m at 15 cm, and ±0.1–0.15 m at 5–7.5 cm. This is simple arithmetic; no study measuring width error against GSD was found. Since many Beirut sidewalks are 0.8–2 m wide, 30 cm satellite imagery is good for presence or absence and for rough polygons on wide promenades, but not for accessibility-grade width classes. Vantor's (formerly Maxar's) "15 cm HD" product does not change this. It is 30 cm imagery enhanced by proprietary processing, not native 15 cm capture ([SatNews](https://news.satnews.com/2020/11/12/maxar-debuts-15-cm-hd-offering-high-clarity-from-commercial-satellite-imagery/)). Dense Beirut adds failure modes US benchmarks do not have. Tall buildings lean over sidewalks in off-nadir scenes. Cars parked on the sidewalk hide the curb line, so the imagery shows the clear width at that moment rather than the built width. Curb-less shared streets in the old quarters show no visible boundary between sidewalk and road. Ordering near-nadir scenes captured near midday in summer reduces lean and shadow but does not eliminate them.

Imagery is cheap; licences are the real constraint. Commercial 30 cm archive imagery costs about **$19/km² (tasking $29/km²), and 15 cm HD costs $25/km² archive, both with a 1 km² minimum** through SkyWatch ([SkyWatch](https://skywatch.com/data)). Pléiades Neo 30 cm archive costs about $22.50/km² with a 25 km² minimum through LandInfo ([LandInfo, Jan 2026](https://landinfo.com/wp-content/uploads/Imagery_LandInfo_202601-1.pdf)). Assuming roughly 100 km² per metropolitan area, one 30 cm coverage costs about $2,000–2,500 before any licence uplift for derived products. Free alternatives are limited. OpenAerialMap holds a **12 cm CC-BY drone orthomosaic of the Beirut port blast zone** and CC BY-NC Maxar and Planet post-blast scenes at 0.4–0.8 m ([OpenAerialMap API](https://api.openaerialmap.org/meta?bbox=35.09,33.05,36.63,34.69&limit=100)). Google's terms prohibit tracing from its imagery and using it to "train, test, validate or fine-tune" machine-learning models ([Google Maps Platform terms](https://cloud.google.com/archive/terms/maps-platform/maps-analytics-service-terms-20251204)). Esri allows tracing to create vector data, but its machine-learning terms are unverified ([OSM Wiki](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Template:Esri_image)). The Army's Directorate of Geographic Affairs is responsible for national aerial photography, but no product list, GSD or access route is published ([Lebanese Army DGA](https://www.lebarmy.gov.lb/en/node/3984)).

For obstructions, overhead imagery can see tree crowns, parked cars, roofed kiosks and large café terraces. It cannot reliably see poles, signposts, bollards or generator cabinets at satellite resolution, and it shows where a tree's canopy is, not where its trunk blocks the path. Tile2Net has no object classes at all. Drone imagery would close much of the gap. A 2020 PPK fixed-wing survey of the blast zone achieved **3 cm geolocation** ([GIM International](https://www.gim-international.com/content/news/the-beirut-explosion-uav-mapping-as-a-first-step-to-recovery)). But drones are currently not an option, as the legal section explains. The only documented airborne LiDAR in Lebanon is a 2018 archaeological survey north of Beirut ([Rom et al. 2020](https://edoc.ku.de/id/eprint/25372/1/Rom_etal_2020.pdf)), so no city-scale 3D source exists for estimating curb height from the air.

## Street-level imagery is the workhorse, if the team captures its own

Street-level imagery sees what satellites miss: curbs, poles, generators, shop displays and the sidewalk surface itself. In Lebanon, though, the obvious source is both legally and technically weak. The sources disagree on Google Street View coverage. The 2018 launch covered only 41 heritage sites and campuses captured by backpack Trekker ([Arabian Business](https://www.arabianbusiness.com/industries/technology/404221-google-street-view-launches-lebanon-collection)). Several sources report that **road coverage first appeared in April 2024**, captured between 2021 and 2023 with a low-quality camera across Beirut, Mount Lebanon and the Beqaa, producing "distorted and pixelated images" ([The961](https://the961.com/google-street-view-is-working-in-lebanon/); [VirtualStreets](https://virtualstreets.org/index.php/2025/01/01/2024-in-google-street-view-a-look-back-and-forward/)). Even where coverage exists, Google's terms restrict bulk downloading and building indices or datasets from imagery. Its own example of a prohibited use is building an index of tree locations, which is close to an obstruction inventory ([Helbich et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11504); [ConductAtlas](https://conductatlas.com/platform/google-maps/google-maps-platform-terms-of-service/no-scraping-or-content-extraction/)). Price is not the barrier, at $7 per 1,000 Static API images after 10,000 free per month ([Google pricing](https://developers.google.com/maps/documentation/streetview/usage-limits?authuser=1)). The licence is. Google Street View is usable only for human labelling inside an embedded viewer, which is the Project Sidewalk model, and only for presence or absence, given its age and quality.

Mapillary is the proven open alternative and was the main imagery source for the MIT/AUB study. Its images are **CC BY-SA 4.0** ([OSM Wiki](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Mapillary)). Its free API returns machine-detected map features such as utility poles, benches, hydrants, store signs and traffic signs, which can seed an obstruction layer at no cost ([Mapillary API](https://www.mapillary.com/developer/api-documentation)). Beirut imagery exists from 2019 and from post-blast community capture in 2020 ([HOT mailing list](https://lists.openstreetmap.org/pipermail/hot/2020-August/015312.html)). Coverage is community-driven, though, and its extent was not quantified in this research. Querying the `mly1_public` vector tiles is the first desk task. The licence status of detections used outside OSM is ambiguous. No KartaView or Panoramax coverage in Lebanon was found. Panoramax is still worth knowing as a self-hostable open platform.

The decisive advantage goes to teams that capture their own imagery. Consumer 8K 360° cameras cost **$300–590** (GoPro MAX 2 launched at $499.99, Insta360 X5 at $549.99) ([Imaging Resource](https://old.imaging-resource.com/news/2025/09/23/gopro-max-2-launched-price-8k-360-video-features-and-where-to-buy); [FrontDeskReview](https://frontdeskreview.com/gear/360-cameras/insta360-x5-vs-gopro-max-2/)). Mapillary's guidance is 24–25 fps 360° video at 5.6K–8K, which generates about 500 MB per minute ([Mapillary Help](https://help.mapillary.com/hc/en-us/articles/360012674619)). In Beirut, walking or slow two-wheeler capture is better than car-roof capture, because a car on the carriageway sees the sidewalk from behind the parked cars that occupy it. Own capture also fixes the camera height, and that is the scale cue on which metric width estimation depends.

The computer-vision methods are maturing quickly. **UrbanVGGT (2026)** estimates sidewalk width from a single street-view image by combining segmentation, feed-forward 3D reconstruction, ground-plane fitting and camera-height calibration. It reports **0.252 m mean absolute error, with 95.5% of estimates within 0.5 m** in Washington DC, and the authors name metric scale calibration as the most critical component ([arXiv 2603.22531](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22531)). A TU Wien thesis that combined segmentation with monocular depth reached only **64% agreement on width classes** across 8,525 Vienna images, and it worked best where the curb was visible ([TU Wien](https://repositum.tuwien.at/handle/20.500.12708/229239?mode=full)). Segmentation models trained on Mapillary Vistas v2 already include sidewalk, curb, curb cut, pole, utility pole and barrier classes ([Mapillary blog](https://blog.mapillary.com/update/2021/01/18/vistas-2-dataset.html)). Lebanon's characteristic obstructions are not standard classes in any dataset: generator cabinets, vendor stalls, shop spill-over, and cars parked on the sidewalk. They need a locally fine-tuned detector. HeiGIT's 2026 pipeline shows that text-prompted segmentation (SAM3) can bootstrap labels, though it published no accuracy figures ([HeiGIT](https://heigit.org/measuring-what-you-never-notice-a-computer-vision-approach-to-accessible-paths/)). Obstruction detection is the weakest-studied piece. Obstacles make up only 6.4% of verified Project Sidewalk labels ([ArchUp](https://archup.net/pedestrian-sidewalk-barriers/)), and no Lebanese or MENA street-view sidewalk study exists. Expect the error rates in Beirut to be worse than the US benchmarks until the models are calibrated against local tape-measured ground truth.

Crowdsourced virtual auditing is the human-in-the-loop complement. **Project Sidewalk reached 92% label accuracy over 3,000+ miles of streets in Washington DC** ([arXiv 2206.13677](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.13677)), and it has run in 35 cities across 8 countries, including Mexico and Ecuador ([PacTrans](https://depts.washington.edu/pactrans/?p=8749)). That 92% depends on consensus validation. Single-labeller precision was only **20.5% for missing curb ramps and 47.5% for obstacles** ([Saha et al. 2019](https://makeabilitylab.cs.washington.edu/media/talks/Saha_ProjectSidewalkAWebBasedCrowdsourcingToolForCollectingSidewalkAccessibilityDataAtScale_2019.pdf)). The code is open source ([GitHub](https://github.com/ProjectSidewalk/SidewalkWebpage)), but it is built around Google Street View, so pointing it at Mapillary or the team's own imagery is an engineering task.

## Field measurement remains the only source for curb height

Some attributes have no remote substitute: curb height, ramp slope, cross slope, surface defects and the narrowest clear width at a pinch point. Tape, measuring wheel and laser distance meter remain the reference instruments. Smartphone LiDAR speeds up documentation without replacing them. The iPhone Pro achieves **3–6 cm relative accuracy on small objects** ([ISPRS 2022](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XLIII-B2-2022-303/2022/isprs-archives-XLIII-B2-2022-303-2022.pdf)), and a 2025 real-time pipeline kept errors for objects within 5 m often below 0.5 m RMSE ([arXiv 2512.22392](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.22392)). But a US DOT-funded evaluation found inconsistent curb-ramp slopes and significant gaps caused by vegetation and **parked cars** ([ROSAP](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/87091)), which are exactly the conditions on Lebanese sidewalks.

Positioning sets the second accuracy ceiling. Phone GNSS errors average around **5–10 m in urban canyons** ([Ng et al. 2021](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/handle/10397/92730?mode=full)). That is enough to identify the street segment but not the side of the street. Lebanon has an underused asset here: **LEB-CORS, a 13-station Army-run network-RTK service advertising about 3 cm real-time accuracy** ([lebcors.army.gov.lb](https://lebcors.army.gov.lb); [LBCI](https://www.lbcgroup.tv/news/lebanon-news/691386/lebanese-army-launches-online-network-to-facilitate-geographical-surve/en)). Its subscription terms are not published, and civilian or academic access must be confirmed. With it, an Emlid Reach RX rover (about $2,199; 7 mm + 1 ppm horizontal) gives centimetre-level points ([reach.dog](https://reach.dog/shop/advexure/products/emlid-reach-rx)). Without it, the team needs a pair of Reach RS3 units at about $2,999 each, one as a local base ([Advexure](https://advexure.com/collections/emlid-rtk-gnss-receivers-and-surveying-tools/products/emlid-reach-rs3)). RTK will lose its fix under balconies and in narrow souks, so record fix status on every point. For audit attributes, referencing each observation by segment ID plus a wheel-measured distance from the segment start is cheaper and more robust than RTK on every point.

Handheld SLAM LiDAR is the high-end option. Walked along the sidewalk, it captures width, curb height and every obstruction in a single pass. Mid-range RTK-capable units such as the CHCNAV RS10 (about €24,000; under 1 cm relative, under 5 cm absolute) and XGRIDS Lixel L2 Pro (about 1 cm relative, 3 cm absolute with RTK) suit university budgets ([3Dsurvey](https://3dsurvey.si/7-slam-scanners-put-to-the-test/); [Radiance Fields](https://radiancefields.com/xgrids/lixel-l2-pro)). A 2026 comparison for sidewalk HD mapping found handheld SLAM the most robust platform where GNSS is degraded ([Sensors 26(5)](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/26/5/1480)). Vehicle systems such as the Trimble MX50 position to 2 cm ([Trimble](https://www.monsenengineering.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/022516-715-en-US_Trimble_MobileMappingSolutions_ProductComparison_USL_0224_LRsec.pdf)), but from the carriageway they cannot see past parked cars. No Lebanese provider of mobile mapping was confirmed, and no sidewalk-specific km/day throughput figure exists for any field method. Point-cloud processing, not walking time, is likely to be the bottleneck.

For the field tooling, **KoboToolbox's free nonprofit plan allows 5,000 submissions per month** and supports Arabic/English forms ([KoboToolbox](https://www.kobotoolbox.org/blog/supporting-future-growth-and-accessibility-introducing-kobotoolboxs-new-plan-model)). Put the Arabic label first, because right-to-left rendering depends on the first visible script ([Kobo forum](https://community.kobotoolbox.org/t/language-alignment-to-be-in-right-arabic-language/47322)). QField (free tier, Pro at €12 per month) is the better choice when collectors must draw or edit lines offline ([QFieldCloud](https://qfield.cloud/pricing)). The data model should follow the **OpenSidewalks schema v0.3 (January 2026)**. It is OSM-compatible: sidewalk edges, curb nodes and crossings carry width, incline, surface and tactile paving, and adjacent points cover poles, trees, bollards and benches. Lebanon-specific fields can be added under its `ext:` prefix ([OpenSidewalks](https://github.com/OpenSidewalks/OpenSidewalks-Schema)). The team should record total width, minimum and typical clear width, curb height, surface condition and parking encroachment per segment. For each obstruction it should record type, permanence, occupied width and remaining clear width, so that effective-width profiles can be computed without full 3D scans. MAPS-style segmentation, which starts a new unit each time the auditor crosses the street, gives a validated audit structure. **75% of MAPS subscales showed good or excellent inter-rater reliability**, and on-street and online audits took a similar mean time (22 ± 12 minutes per unit) ([Millstein et al. 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3728214/); [MAPS-Global](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7844998/)).

## Drones are grounded, and cameras need letters and judgement

The legal environment rules out one method and constrains the others. Lebanon has no dedicated drone law. The Lebanese Army is the operative permit authority, and SMEX's legal expert says municipalities lack that power ([SMEX, Oct 2025](https://smex.org/drones-in-lebanon-who-can-fly-them-and-are-they-legal/)). On **1 March 2026 the Army suspended all drone flight permits nationwide "until further notice"** ([LBCI](https://v7cdn.lbcgroup.tv/news/lebanon-news/908645/lebanese-army-suspends-drone-permits-nationwide-amid-regional-tensions/en)), and no report of the suspension being lifted was found. Drones have been seized at Tripoli port this year ([Annahar](https://www.annahar.com/lebanon/335825/الجمارك-اللبنانية-تضبط-درونات-ودراجات-رباعية-مقصوصة-ومعاد-تلحيمها-في-طرابلس)), and hostile drones are active over Beirut. A civilian research drone would therefore risk confiscation, detention, or being mistaken for a hostile aircraft. The plan should assume no drones.

Ground-level photography is legal in principle, but practically sensitive. Journalists have been detained in Dahiyeh for filming without Hezbollah permission ([The National](https://www.thenationalnews.com/mena/lebanon/journalists-freed-after-being-detained-in-hezbollah-stronghold-1.1250746)), and in 2025 a TV crew's footage was deleted in Burj al-Barajneh ([SKeyes](https://www.skeyesmedia.org/en/News/Reports/26-02-2025/12321)). No written list of no-photo zones was found. A walking 360° survey should carry university and Lebanese-partner letters in Arabic, ideally with a letter from the Beirut Governor's office. It should keep cameras off military and security sites, embassies and party offices, and skip Dahiyeh and the Palestinian camps unless local gatekeepers have agreed in advance. Law 81/2018 covers identifying photographs and can ground prosecutions, even though it has **no independent data protection authority** ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/?c=LB&t=law); [SMEX](https://smex.org/drones-in-lebanon-who-can-fly-them-and-are-they-legal/)). Blurring faces and plates at ingestion, keeping raw imagery encrypted, and publishing only blurred imagery and derived features is the defensible standard. In practice the binding rules will be the funder's and the university's. AUB's IRB reviews all human-subjects research by AUB personnel wherever it takes place ([AUB IRB](https://www.aub.edu.lb/irb/Pages/Aboutus.aspx)). An infrastructure audit should therefore seek a formal exemption ruling. Shopkeeper interviews or walk-alongs with disabled pedestrians will need full review.

Security frames everything. Lebanon is under a **US Level 4 "Do Not Travel" advisory** that warns of strikes anywhere, including Beirut ([US Embassy, Oct 2026](https://lb.usembassy.gov/travel-advisory-lebanon-october-2026/)). Strikes on 8 April 2026 hit central Beirut without warning and killed 357 people nationwide ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8_April_2026_Israeli_attacks_on_Lebanon)). Fieldwork should be led by Lebanese team members, run in short daylight sessions, and have suspension triggers tied to evacuation warnings. Many foreign universities will not clear travel at all, which argues for funding a local field team rather than flying one in.

The legal framework offers no width benchmark. Building Law 646/2004, Traffic Law 243/2012 (which fines sidewalk parking) and disability Law 220/2000 all exist. Decree 7194/2011 covers new public-use buildings, not streets. The research found no statutory minimum sidewalk width ([Executive Magazine](https://www.executive-magazine.com/business-all/real-estate/lebanon-disabled-buildings); [Civil Society Knowledge Centre](https://civilsociety-centre.org/paper/lebanon-2004-construction-law-inside-parliamentary-debates)). What does exist is a ready-made legal typology of obstructions. **Beirut Governor's Warning No. 11866/2026 (2 November 2026)** targets tables, chairs, tents, umbrellas, planters, barriers, goods displays, stalls, beverage machines, private space-reserving obstacles, sidewalk parking and unlicensed valet operations. Field inspections, with closure under red seal as a sanction, begin on **16 November 2026** ([Libnanews](https://en.libnanews.com/beirut-abboud-sets-violators-free-sidewalks-global-voices)). Coding obstructions against those categories makes the dataset legally meaningful. The campaign also creates a natural before-and-after experiment. The cost is that shopkeepers will be warier of anyone photographing their frontage while it runs.

The table shows two tiers that complement each other. The cheap, scalable sources (OSM, curblines, satellite, crowd labels) settle *where* sidewalks are and whether they exist. Only own street-level capture, manual audit and SLAM settle *how wide, how high, and what is in the way*.

## A four-phase methodology

**Phase 0, permissions and partnerships (about the first two months).** Partner with a Lebanese university, ideally AUB, which holds the curblines, co-authored the MIT study and runs the Urban Observatory. Formally request the 2010 curbline and light-pole layers, the Community Streets per-street table, and Urban Lab footprints, and negotiate redistribution terms. Ask the Directorate of Geographic Affairs about LEB-CORS access and any recent orthophotos. Write to the Beirut Governor's office and to the municipal engineering departments of Beirut, Tripoli, Saida, Jounieh, Zahle and Tyre for as-built drawings and a field letter. Get an IRB exemption ruling and a security protocol with suspension triggers. If a local team is already in place, a quick walking 360° baseline capture of a few commercial corridors such as Hamra, Mar Mikhael and Gemmayzeh **before 16 November 2026** would preserve a before-image of the enforcement campaign. That baseline cannot be recovered later.

**Phase 1, desk-built base layer (in parallel).** Split OSM centrelines into one segment per side of the street, following the MIT/AUB approach of doubling arterials and linking them with crossings. Then compute built widths for Municipal Beirut from the curb-to-footprint distance. Query Mapillary's coverage tiles for every target city and harvest its pole, bench, hydrant and sign detections. Buy near-nadir, summer, midday 30 cm archive imagery for the pilot areas, with derived-product rights confirmed in writing. Run pretrained Tile2Net to get draft sidewalk polygons, which will need correcting. Every attribute produced in this phase is a prior, flagged by `ext:method` in the OpenSidewalks-based schema, and none is a measurement.

**Phase 2, pilot census with ground truth.** Choose two or three contrasting neighbourhoods, for example a Beirut commercial district, a post-blast residential area and a Tripoli or Saida old-city fabric, and survey every segment in them. Walking teams capture 360° video at a fixed, recorded camera height. Paired auditors fill Kobo or QField forms with wheel and laser measurements of total width, minimum clear width, curb height and surface condition. Every obstruction is coded against the Warning 11866/2026 categories plus Lebanon-specific classes (generators, parked cars, A/C drains, building steps intruding onto the sidewalk), with a photo. A 5% subsample is re-measured with RTK, or with a rented SLAM unit, as independent ground truth, and 10% is double-audited to report ICC or kappa per attribute. The pilot has three outputs: the dataset itself, a labelled local training set, and measured error rates.

**Phase 3, model calibration and scale-up in Beirut.** Fine-tune Tile2Net on the corrected pilot masks. Fine-tune a Vistas-trained segmentation model and a YOLO detector on the local obstruction classes. Run UrbanVGGT-style width estimation on the team's own imagery, calibrated with the known camera height. Score every model output against the Phase 2 ground truth and publish the errors. Run a Project Sidewalk-style validation interface with three or more student labellers per item, accepting only consensus labels. Repeat imagery capture on a subset of corridors at different times of day and week, because parked cars, vendors and café seating are temporal phenomena that a single snapshot misrepresents.

**Phase 4, stratified survey beyond Beirut.** For Tripoli, Saida, Jounieh, Zahle and Tyre, where no prior data exists, draw a stratified random sample of about 5–15% of segments by municipality, street class and frontage type. Collect it with the Phase 2 protocol, and use the Phase 3 models to extend estimates to unsampled segments with stated uncertainty. Publish in OpenSidewalks/OSM-compatible GeoJSON and GeoPackage, with blurred imagery uploaded to Mapillary or a self-hosted Panoramax under CC BY-SA. Contribute geometry and widths back to OSM so the next team does not start from 32 ways.

## Conclusion

The data gap in Lebanon is not one of technology. Every needed method is commercially or academically available. What is missing are labels, licences and a safe way to put people and cameras on the street. Methods developed in US cities assume municipal GIS for training, drive-by Street View and drone access, and Lebanon has none of these. So the order of operations is reversed. Field ground truth has to come *first*, because it is the only way to calibrate satellite and street-level models to a city where parked cars sit on the curb and generators fill the frontage. The most important early investment is therefore a carefully measured pilot of a few neighbourhoods. A citywide purchase of imagery or hardware can wait.

Two features of 2026 make the work distinctive. Beirut now has a legal obstruction typology and an enforcement date, which turns a descriptive inventory into a tool for evaluating policy. And the country's main open asset for sidewalk data is people, not data: the AUB and MIT researchers who have already estimated Beirut's sidewalks, the OSM volunteers who captured post-blast imagery, and the Army's underused CORS network. The cheapest gains come from negotiating with them. The remaining uncertainties all need direct enquiry before budgets are fixed: LEB-CORS access terms, Mapillary's actual coverage, whether the drone suspension is lifted, and the licence on the MIT/AUB table.
